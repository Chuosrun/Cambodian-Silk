The traditional Cambodian golden silk production relies on meticulous manual labor, transforming voracious caterpillars into fine, durable textiles. The lifecycle spans several weeks of careful cultivation followed by precise extraction techniques.

Cultivation and Feeding

The process begins by placing newly hatched silkworms into wide, flat bamboo trays to keep them safe and contained.

Farmers feed the insects a constant, heavy diet of freshly chopped, young mulberry leaves, which provide the exact nutrients needed for silk production.

The worms eat relentlessly for roughly 25 to 30 days, rapidly expanding in size and shedding their skin multiple times until they become thick, translucent caterpillars ready to pupate.

Spinning the Golden Cocoons

Once fully mature and bursting with liquid silk, the caterpillars are gently moved onto bundles of dried twigs or woven nesting frames.

They immediately begin secreting liquid fibroin protein from their salivary glands, spinning it tightly around themselves in a continuous figure-eight pattern.

Unlike the white silk common in other regions, traditional Cambodian silkworms naturally produce vibrant, golden-yellow casings.

After the caterpillars fully enclose themselves and transition into pupae, the hardened cocoons are manually plucked from the branches and gathered into baskets.

Boiling and Extraction

The cocoons are quickly moved to a charcoal or wood-fired stove and submerged in a pot of boiling water.

This critical heat treatment serves two purposes: it prevents the pupa inside from maturing and chewing through the silk, and it melts the sericin—a natural gummy resin acting as a hard glue.

By dissolving the sericin, the delicate microscopic fibers can be safely unwound without snapping or tangling.

Reeling and Final Processing

While the cocoons bob in the hot water, a weaver uses a wooden tool to agitate them, catching the loose ends of the microscopic filaments.

Because a single filament is far too fragile to use alone, threads from dozens of cocoons are caught and twisted together to form a single, robust strand.

This strand is continuously hand-reeled onto a traditional wooden spinning wheel.

The resulting skeins of raw silk are repeatedly washed to strip away any residual gum, hung out to air dry in the sun, and sometimes dyed before being loaded onto a traditional loom.
